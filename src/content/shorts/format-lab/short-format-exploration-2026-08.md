# Short Format Lab — August 2026

## Recommendation

Build a recognizable short-form universe around one promise:

> **HUMAN REVIEW REQUIRED**  
> Real artifacts. Real decisions. Real consequences.

The opportunity is not to make synthetic demos look more real. It is to wrap
real work in an entertaining form. The factual core should be inspectable; the
comedy, metaphor, graphics, and generated imagery can be openly stylized.

## Why This Direction

- YouTube's creator guidance emphasizes unique lived experience and warns that
  audiences can see through a manufactured persona.
- TikTok's 2026 trend material emphasizes reality, participation, comments as a
  creative surface, and evidence over polished brand performance.
- YouTube now makes meaningful AI-generation disclosure more visible in Shorts.
- Aaron's own channel notes say the same thing more bluntly: emotion beats cold
  information. A useful fact needs tension, disbelief, or a human consequence.
- The just-finished Codex demo supplied a perfect authentic pilot: every
  technical check passed, yet the music felt cheap and the interface looked
  fake. That is a real failure with a memorable lesson.

The working rule for every episode:

1. Show at least one real artifact.
2. Reveal one real decision.
3. Land on one real consequence.
4. Never stage a generated interface as a literal screen recording.
5. If generated media is realistic, label it; if it is illustrative, make the
   stylization obvious.
6. End on one sentence the viewer can repeat.

### 1. Demo CSI / AI Passed QA

**Recurring premise:** Autopsy an AI result that was technically valid but
humanly wrong.

**Pilot:** `AI Passed QA. Human Hated It.` The Codex recreation passed 1080p,
audio-loudness, and decode checks. Aaron watched it and immediately found the
two things the checklist could not measure: taste and trust.

**Visual language:** Actual video frames, the real QA report, grease-pencil
circles, evidence tags, deadpan stamps, and large editorial typography. No fake
software chrome.

**Share trigger:** Honest self-critique. It turns an embarrassing miss into a
useful test for every AI builder.

### 2. My AI Employee Is on Probation

**Recurring premise:** Give an AI worker a real role, bounded authority, a
deadline, and an explicit renewal decision.

**Possible first arc:** Four days to improve blog distribution with zero paid
budget. Each episode reveals one proposed action, one approval or rejection,
and one measured outcome. Do not pre-script the outcome.

**Visual language:** Employee card, probation timer, approval/rejection stamp,
real public artifacts, and an anonymized scorecard. Treat it like a workplace
reality show, not an AI-product commercial.

**Share trigger:** The audience develops a view about whether the agent should
be retained. The story creates a reason to return.

### 3. Would You Let AI Cross This Line?

**Recurring premise:** The viewer classifies one task as `AUTO`, `APPROVAL`, or
`HUMAN ONLY`, then Aaron reveals his real boundary and why.

**Pilot example:** Local, reversible prototype change versus a change to a
system of record. The risk is not how many lines the agent edits; it is which
authority boundary the change crosses.

**Visual language:** One physical red line, task cards, three answer zones, a
countdown, and a dry verdict sound. The comments become part of the next episode.

**Share trigger:** A concrete disagreement people can answer before they know
Aaron's position.

### 4. Agent Arena

**Recurring premise:** Give two or three agents the same difficult task and
score evidence, tradeoffs, and false confidence—not just visual polish.

**Example:** Which agent can turn a messy launch memo into the safest execution
plan? Publish the prompt, rubric, and raw outputs so the competition remains
credible.

**Visual language:** Printed prompt card, race lanes, timers, evidence tokens,
and a fixed score board. The actual outputs are receipts, not background decor.

### 5. AI Office Sitcom

**Recurring premise:** A 15–25 second sketch based on something an agent really
did. The recurring cast is `PM`, `Agent`, `Reviewer`, and `User`.

**Example:** The agent says “done” three times. The reviewer asks, “What did you
verify?” Thirty-seven green checks fall away because none of them represent a
user test.

**Constraint:** Never write a generic AI joke first and search for evidence
later. Start with the real failure.

### 6. One Model, One Physical Metaphor

**Recurring premise:** Explain one AI-native operating model with one memorable
physical device or miniature world.

**Examples:**

- `10× here. Still 1× here.` — a fast conveyor belt jams at a gate labelled
  `JUDGMENT`.
- `New motor, old belt.` — an electric motor drives a steam-era approval system.
- `Blast radius.` — autonomy blocks grow until the deploy block knocks over the
  whole desk.

This is the most visually ownable format, but every metaphor must resolve into
a real decision Aaron has made.

## Recommended First Three Pilots

The first batch should test three different propagation mechanisms rather than
three cosmetic treatments of the same idea.

1. **AI Passed QA. Human Hated It.** — self-deprecation and trust.
2. **My AI Employee Is on Probation: Day 0.** — serial narrative.
3. **Would You Let AI Cross This Line?** — prediction and comments.

`Agent Arena` should be the fourth pilot after the scorecard and evidence-link
format are ready; it has more production overhead and a higher cherry-picking
risk.

## Format Grammar

- **Duration:** 18–28 seconds by default.
- **Frame zero:** the conflict or receipt, never a logo or topic label.
- **Beat shape:** receipt → tension → reversal → operating rule.
- **Text:** index the idea; do not duplicate every spoken word.
- **Evidence:** show actual files, output, source cards, physical artifacts, or
  genuine captures. If a real capture is unavailable, use editorial typography
  rather than a simulated product UI.
- **Aspect ratios:** author a 9:16 primary timeline, then create a deliberately
  recomposed 16:9 X cut from the same beat map.
- **Brand device:** one recurring `HUMAN REVIEW REQUIRED` stamp at the payoff,
  not persistent decorative chrome.
- **Voice:** deadpan and specific. Avoid generic creator hype.

## Music Strategy

Use the blog's Eleven Music `music_v2` pipeline and generate a new 24–30 second
micro-score for each pilot. Do not cut a random section from a long ambient
track and do not return to hand-built UI pulses.

Three sonic families are enough for the pilot batch:

1. **Forensic deadpan:** low cello, sparse felt piano, muted viola pizzicato,
   warm room air.
2. **Probation reality:** restrained acoustic pulse, dry low percussion, one
   recurring bass motif, no TV-show parody.
3. **Decision game:** small chamber plucks and a controlled low pulse that stops
   cleanly at the verdict.

Recommended musical structure:

`0–2s hook → 2–9s establish → 9–16s reversal → 16–22s payoff → 22–24s dry button`

Generate three candidates and select by listening. Preserve the exact prompt,
model, song ID, duration, hashes, edit decisions, and rights review. Target
approximately `-16 LUFS` with true peak at or below `-1 dBTP`; duck under voice.
Use at most two sound effects, and only when they land on a real action or
punchline.

## Pilot Evaluation

Judge the three pilots on different jobs:

- Did the opening receipt stop the scroll?
- Did viewers reach the reversal and rewatch it?
- Did they share or save the operating rule?
- Did comments contain real decisions or only generic praise?
- Could a viewer describe the series premise without seeing the title?

Do not optimize around a generic posting-volume rule. Release one deliberate
video at a time, wrap it in a builder story on X, and use actual audience data
to decide which format becomes the series.
