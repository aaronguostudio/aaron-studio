# A Greek Trip I Couldn't Have Taken the Same Way Without AI

Trips are supposed to begin with departure. Ours began with a problem the itinerary had never mentioned.

We were close to boarding when a guardianship-related document stopped one person in our party. We had paperwork, but the possible proof was buried among residency and immigration materials—documents that looked official, yet might not answer the question in front of us. There was no time for a leisurely search and no page in the plan called “what to do when this happens.”

I opened GPT. Not to ask, “Can we board?” That would have been the wrong question. I asked which kinds of records, among the documents already on our phones, might be relevant evidence. It helped us sort the pile, compare descriptions, and find a lead. It did not decide whether any document was acceptable. It could not. That belonged to the carrier and the people responsible for the rule.

I thought about that moment for the rest of the trip. I could have gone to Greece without AI. But I would not have taken the same trip. Once plans met reality, it became the quickest way back into whatever context I had lost: a document, a local question, a codebase, a new technical idea, and eventually this piece of writing.

That is also the line worth keeping. For rules, documents, safety, money, or deadlines, AI can help someone get their bearings. It is not the authority. Australia’s [Smartraveller guidance on AI travel planning](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/news-and-updates/travel-planning-ai) makes the same practical case: begin there if it helps, then check important information with the official source.

## Maps told us where. GPT helped with the rest.

After that, the questions became smaller and more ordinary. That is where the habit really showed itself.

In a new city, the day is full of tiny unfinished sentences. What is this place? What did we just see? Which restaurant is right for tonight? How do I ask this without sounding stiff? Is this price a conversation, or is it simply the price? I used GPT for Greek classics, for the places we visited, for restaurants, and for the small act of finding the right words with a merchant.

Its value was simpler: the day broke less often into research chores. I was not trying to build a perfect list before leaving the hotel. I was trying to stay with the day while the small questions kept arriving.

Only afterward did I notice something slightly strange: Google Maps was open constantly, but I never opened Google Search. Maps still did the job it has always done—where are we, and how do we get there? GPT had quietly taken over another job: holding a conversation around the question I had not fully formed yet.

That is not a verdict on search. When I need a primary source, a comparison, or a rule that matters, I still search and verify. But not every question needs a page of results. Some need a little context, a little language, and someone—or something—to help turn “I’m not sure” into a next step.

## At night, DrumNext came back with me

By day, we travelled. At night, back in the room, the trip opened a second clock.

The ship’s Starlink connection meant I could return to DrumNext. It was never the purpose of the holiday. It was simply a piece of work I had brought with me, still alive, still asking for decisions. The European evening also happened to overlap more naturally with my team’s working hours, so there was room to talk, review, and move something forward without pulling the whole day into work.

DrumNext started as a metronome for drummers. The version I was working on is trying to become more than that. A metronome can tell a player where the beat is. Practice has a more interesting job: it has to take the beat away at the right time, increase the difficulty, remember a useful setup, and show whether the player came back.

That is what the new work was about: custom practice setups, internal-timing and random-mute exercises, speed work, saved presets, practice history, and weekly goals. Beneath the visible parts were the less glamorous things that make a feature real—persistence, sync, and tests.

Before AI, I would have waited until I was home, back at my desk, with a clean day in front of me. On the ship I did not have that luxury. I might have an hour or two. Normally, much of that time would disappear into remembering: where the feature stood, what had changed, what the edge cases were, and which half-formed thought was worth pursuing.

Now I could re-enter more gently. I could ask for a feature trace, a restatement of a requirement, a list of cases I might be missing. Claude Code could work through parts of the repository and its checks. The judgment was still mine: what DrumNext should become, what the practice flow should feel like, and what was ready to belong in the product. But the distance between a thought and a responsible piece of work had become shorter.

That is the quieter version of what I wrote about in [The One-Person Project](/blogs/one-person-project-ai-coding). AI can widen the ground one person can cover. It does not remove the need to know where the ground ends.

## A holiday can have two clocks

I do not want to turn this into a case for working through a holiday. A laptop can take over a trip very easily. Every quiet moment starts to look like an available hour. Soon, you are physically somewhere new and mentally still at your desk.

On this trip, the pleasure was being able to leave work alone for a while without feeling that it would evaporate. I could spend the day in Greece, return in the evening, make a bounded contribution, speak to the team, and close the computer again.

The time zone made that rhythm possible. AI made it less costly. Neither of them told me what to do with my time. They only made the choice feel less like a trade between two incompatible lives.

## Learning still needs a way in

There was another thread running through the trip. I had been trying to understand DeepSeek Harness more deeply.

From a distance, a framework like that looks like a row of familiar words: agent loop, tools, plugins, configuration, state. Up close, those words are not understanding. The real question is whether I can find a way into the project, ask a better question than I could yesterday, and use the answer in actual work.

GPT was useful as a way in. I could ask it to sketch a map of an unfamiliar concept before I followed it into the code. Claude Code was useful on the other side of that threshold: reading the repository, following its conventions, making changes, running checks. The two tools did not feel interchangeable. One helped me orient myself; the other helped me keep moving once I knew where I was going.

And then there was this essay. The same tools helped me gather a few loose things—a boarding problem, a week in Greece, a product release, a framework I was learning—and notice that they belonged to the same story. They did not supply the experience or the point of view. They helped me find the thread between them. That is the kind of work I built my content system for in [Why I Stopped Writing Blog Posts (And Built an AI Studio Instead)](/blogs/why-i-stopped-writing-and-built-ai-studio).

## The browser tab I never opened

When I think back on the trip, the detail that stays with me is almost comically small: I used Google Maps, but I never used Google Search.

That does not mean search has disappeared. It means that some questions have found a different first door. Instead of beginning with keywords and a row of blue links, I began with a conversation that already held the immediate situation: we were in Greece; we had a preference, a time constraint, a document in front of us, or a product still in motion.

The best moments with AI were not the ones where it seemed clever. They were the ones where it became almost invisible. A question loosened. A document became legible. A sentence found its shape. A feature came back into focus. Then I could return to the person in front of me, the city around me, or the work that was actually worth doing.

The rule I brought home is simple: let AI help find the next step; know who gets to make the final call.

It did not decide whether we could board. It did not decide which restaurant mattered, what DrumNext should become, or whether I should work while travelling. It only made the friction lighter while those decisions were still mine.

That was enough. It left more room for the holiday itself—and, when I chose to open the laptop, for the work worth carrying with me.
