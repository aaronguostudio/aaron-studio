# Video Brief: Should You Read AI-Generated Code?

This is the 6–10 minute YouTube essay for the published article. The approved
one-minute Clean Indigo film remains a visual prototype and trailer, not the
final long-form treatment.

## Target Audience

Developers, tech leads, engineering managers, maintainers, and AI-native
builders who are producing more code than they can comfortably review.

## Desired Emotion

Intellectual relief followed by practical confidence. The viewer should see
that the public argument is not a choice between discipline and speed, and
leave with a clear risk-based decision rule.

## Core Promise

The video will fairly reconstruct Mitchell Hashimoto's and Robert C. Martin's
apparently opposite positions, explain why the community argument became so
intense, show how real organizations are adapting, and land a practical rule:
review depth follows what a change is allowed to make true.

## Title/Thumbnail Expectation

- Recommended title: `Should You Read AI-Generated Code?`
- Thumbnail: `AI WROTE IT / DO YOU READ IT?`
- Opening obligation: establish the two opposing answers in the first ten
  seconds, then reveal their shared commitment to accountability before 0:30.

## High-Shock Facts

- Mitchell Hashimoto answered the AI-code quality question with: “I read the
  code.” Robert C. Martin says he does not read agent implementation, yet both
  define the human as accountable.
- OpenAI reported an agent-first greenfield product with roughly one million
  lines and about 1,500 merged pull requests without manually written
  implementation code; this remains a company-reported experiment.
- Cloudflare uses up to seven specialized AI reviewers and still assigns review
  depth by risk tier.
- Godot says AI reduced the cost of generating pull requests without increasing
  the supply of qualified human reviewers.
- A five-line write into a system of record can deserve more scrutiny than a
  thousand lines of disposable interface code.

## Hook Type

Apparent contradiction: two respected engineers give opposite operational
answers while claiming the same responsibility.

## Story Structure

Cold contradiction → reconstruct both positions → expose shared ground → map
the community disagreement → show industry experiments → introduce Aaron's
critical-system and system-of-record boundary → give a three-level operating
rule → answer the bureaucracy and learning objections → consequence payoff.

## Retention Beat Map

- 0:00 — Two respected engineers, opposite answers.
- 0:25 — They agree on accountability; they disagree on where understanding
  lives.
- 1:40 — Code review produces more than bug reports.
- 2:45 — The community is paying three different costs at different times.
- 3:20 — OpenAI's million-line greenfield experiment.
- 4:20 — Godot names reviewer scarcity as the bottleneck.
- 5:00 — Aaron's critical-system and system-of-record boundary.
- 6:15 — A three-level rule replaces the universal yes/no answer.
- 7:15 — Risk and learning are separate axes.
- Final minute — Four release questions and the ownership payoff.

## What The Video Adds

- The original positions and their strongest versions, rather than only the
  article's conclusion.
- A fair explanation of the community split: maintenance and mental models,
  verification bandwidth, organizational cost, and junior learning.
- Current industry evidence from OpenAI, Cloudflare, and Godot, clearly bounded
  as company reports or governance responses rather than universal proof.
- A simplified three-level operating rule instead of the rejected detailed
  status-field walkthrough.
- A distinction between shipping risk and learning value.

## Spoken Delivery Contract

- Measured, direct, engineering-minded, and conversational.
- Target 6–10 minutes, approximately 1,050–1,170 spoken words.
- Attribute claims in the narration; place compact source labels on evidence
  scenes without turning the film into a citation deck.
- Phrase-level captions only. No wall-of-text subtitles.
- No music or sound effects unless a later listening pass shows a clear need.

  deep indigo, warm black, restrained serif/sans/mono hierarchy.
- Remove `FIELD JOURNAL`, ruled paper, grids, dashboard chrome, persistent dark
