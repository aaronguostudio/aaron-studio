After listening to DHH's conversation with Lex Fridman, I wanted to try Linux.

That was an interesting response. I already had products to work on, projects to move forward with AI, and a writing workflow of my own to improve. An interview had just given me another world to explore. But underneath that excitement was a familiar motivation: I like making things that improve life, make work less frustrating, and bring some enjoyment into running a business.

**I share DHH's enthusiasm for AI because easier implementation gives more of the things I care about a chance to exist.** Who types the code matters less to me than what the resulting product makes possible.

## The satisfaction is in making something useful

DHH distinguishes the desire to build things from attachment to the mechanical work of programming. That distinction resonated with me. [Interview transcript: advice for programmers](https://lexfridman.com/dhh-2-transcript/#chapter8_advice_for_programmers)

I understand the pleasure of writing beautiful code. Care for structure and expression has value of its own. My greatest satisfaction sits closer to the person using the result: an idea becomes usable, a confusing operation becomes straightforward, or someone no longer has to work around the same annoyance every day.

These improvements can be small. They rarely make a product launch exciting, but they shape the experience of an ordinary working day. When I talk about building products that change life, this is often what I mean in practice. Business has to account for costs and results. I also want its software to leave people with less frustration and a little more control over their work.

So my first response to AI taking on more implementation is curiosity about what I can now attempt. The method is changing. The reason I wanted to do the work is still there.

## Some ideas become worth starting

DHH connects his change in AI practice to agents carrying out useful work, beyond code completion. He describes his standards as consistent while the tools changed. That reminded me of my own experience. [Interview transcript: programming with AI agents](https://lexfridman.com/dhh-2-transcript/#chapter2_programming_with_ai_agents)

A small request can carry a surprisingly large implementation burden. Someone has to find the relevant code, understand the conventions, change the interface, connect the data, check the failure cases, and get the result into a usable environment. The request takes one sentence to describe. Starting it is harder. If the expected improvement is modest, leaving it for later can be a sensible decision. When AI takes on some of that work, the calculation can change. A small improvement may deserve a first version without needing to become a large business.

My blog workflow is a concrete example. An idea can now move through research, an outline, a draft, a Chinese adaptation, and editorial review. Those steps have reusable instructions and leave files I can inspect. I described this change in an earlier post. What matters to me now is that a loose thought has a clearer path to a draft worth editing. I can spend more attention on what it actually says and whether it says it clearly.

The workflow can still produce an article that doesn't deserve publication. But the idea has reached a point where I can examine it. Something that might once have felt too cumbersome to start can get a first version, followed by a decision about whether to continue.

## Taste shows up in ordinary decisions

DHH's product questions—who something serves, what it does, and what comes first—felt familiar. [Interview transcript: product and implementation](https://lexfridman.com/dhh-2-transcript/#chapter6_vibe_coding_vs_agentic_engineering)

Taste can sound abstract, as though it belongs to people with an unusual aesthetic gift. I see it in ordinary choices. Does this feature belong in the product? Where should the first version stop? Does a message tell the user what to do next? When something goes wrong, can the person recover without help? Each decision looks small. Together, they shape whether someone wants to keep using the product.

This article requires the same kind of selection. The interview covers many subjects, but I have to decide which ones connect to my own work. Including every topic could leave the reader with a summary and little sense of why I cared. For example, this could easily become another article about judgment becoming more important in the AI era. It would read smoothly and resemble things I have already written. It would still fail to explain why this interview made me want to try Linux or think again about the pleasure of making products. Keeping those particular reactions gives the article a reason to exist. AI can organize the material, propose a structure, and revise sentences. I still need to read the result and decide whether it expresses my meaning.

That is the involvement I want to keep. My hands don't have to remain on the keyboard for me to care about what the thing becomes. Having more possible versions makes choosing among them more consequential.

## Enthusiasm has to survive delivery

Project work also makes it hard for me to treat a generated result as the finish line. I previously wrote about the pressure on QA when code and proposals arrive faster than people can understand and verify the changes. Putting more work into the queue can simply make another group busier.

My trust in AI therefore varies with the situation. A small tool I use myself can reveal problems through quick experiments, and I can set it aside. A system that a team or customer depends on needs stronger evidence around data, permissions, failure handling, and recovery. I'm willing to let AI propose an implementation, including a better approach than the one I had in mind. Someone still has to take responsibility for its effects. People who enjoy writing code aren't behind the times, and people worried about changes to their profession don't owe anyone enthusiasm.

There is also a quieter risk: when starting becomes easier, I can start too many things. Every small tool can create maintenance work. Every automation can eventually fail. If all the implementation time I save turns into time spent looking after more projects, I have given myself a different kind of busyness. I want the freedom to experiment, and the freedom to stop after the experiment has answered its question.

![Every additional project brings an ongoing need for care](imgs/web/03-maintenance-v1.webp)

## Start with something I actually want to use

That brings me back to [Omarchy](https://omarchy.org/), the Linux project associated with DHH. I want to try it and see how far a person's preferences can shape a working environment. This is the beginning of an exploration. I don't have a long-term usage story to tell, and I don't need to turn my curiosity into a complete migration.

It is tempting to come away from DHH asking how to become as capable as he is. That question is so large that it can turn a lively interest into an endless list of prerequisites. A smaller question is more useful to me: which recurring annoyance do I care enough about to solve and live with the result? Let that need guide the learning. When I reach something I don't understand, I have a reason to investigate it.

Before starting another project, I want to be able to say who will use it, which part of their experience should improve, and whether they can return to the old way if it fails. For my writing workflow, that might mean focusing on one problem: can I easily trace a paraphrase in an article back to the interview passage it came from? I can improve that step, check it against an existing draft, and then decide whether to expand it. If it helps, I can continue. If it only demonstrates a technical possibility, I can leave it there.

The interview made me want to get building. Next time I start a project, I want to choose one recurring annoyance, build a small solution, and use it before starting something else. What I look forward to is an ordinary day when that solution earns a place in our work—and the people using it, including me, decide to keep it.
