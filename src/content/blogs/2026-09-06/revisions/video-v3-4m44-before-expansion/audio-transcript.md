## Hook

In my own team, some people don't yet trust AI enough to use it more. Others are already using it enthusiastically. I am closer to that second group. Listening to DHH made me think about the gap between those experiences. How do we turn one person's confidence into a method the rest of the team can examine?

## A reference, not a verdict

What resonated with me was how DHH described the change in his tools. Earlier autocomplete hadn't transformed his work. Agents that could perform tasks and produce results close to his expectations gave him a different experience.

I share that willingness to try again when capabilities change. But his confidence doesn't settle a question inside our project. A colleague who asks whether we can understand a failure deserves an answer from our own work.

Here is a small exercise I would like us to try. It is a proposed practice, not a team success story.

## Put one task on the table

Take a paragraph based on an interview. Our task is to make it clearer while keeping the meaning and the source intact. This is familiar territory for me: my blog workflow already leaves a source record, a draft, and a review trail.

Before asking AI to help, we can agree on what a useful result means. The paragraph should be readable. Its factual statements should still match the source. It should not turn a personal interpretation into a claim about everyone.

Now we have something more specific to discuss than whether AI is good or bad.

## Make the method visible

The person using AI can show the task they gave it, the source it could see, and the changes they accepted. That makes the method available to somebody else.

Consider two statements. DHH describes agents doing useful work. That is a statement about the interview. More of my ideas may now be worth trying. That is my interpretation.

Both can belong in a draft. But if a rewrite turns them into everyone is more productive now, it has added a claim we haven't established. A polished sentence is still open to a specific objection.

## Let both views improve the result

An enthusiastic user can explain what the tool made easier. A colleague with reservations can point to the sentence that needs checking. Neither has to win an argument about AI in general.

If the paragraph improved, keep the method that helped. If a claim drifted, record the failure and change the instructions or review step. A successful writing task would give us evidence about that writing task. It would not establish that the same approach is ready for every system we run.

That distinction matters when we move from a draft we can discard to work other people depend on.

## The standard follows the consequences

I have written before about QA pressure when changes arrive faster than people can understand and verify them. More output can simply move the work into someone else's queue.

I still want to give AI more responsibility. I also want to know what the result affects, how we will notice a failure, and how we can recover. A personal tool and a shared business system need different evidence.

Trust can grow as we learn. The useful question is which part of the work we can now delegate with confidence, and which part needs another check.

## Keep one useful method

The reason I want to do this is simple. I enjoy making products that improve an ordinary working day. If AI can carry more implementation forward, more of those small improvements get a chance to exist.

I don't need everyone to begin with the same excitement. I would like us to finish one useful piece of work together and understand why it helped.

The next time we discuss AI, I want a familiar task on the table, a clear idea of what good looks like, and a result we can inspect together. Then we can decide which method deserves a place in our everyday work.
