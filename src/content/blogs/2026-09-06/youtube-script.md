# Why I Share DHH's Enthusiasm for AI

## [HOOK]

In my own team, some people don't yet trust AI enough to use it more. Others are already using it enthusiastically. I am closer to that second group. Listening to DHH talk with Lex Fridman, I recognized that excitement. Three ideas stayed with me. Together, they helped me explain why I want to give AI more responsibility, and what I want that to mean for our work.

---

## [SLIDE: One: update the judgment — 04-dhh-portrait-v1.png]

First, the tool can change enough to deserve a fresh judgment.

DHH describes a gap between earlier autocomplete and agents that could use tools, check their work, and deliver code much closer to his expectations. His explanation is that his standards remained; the experience changed.

I find that attitude useful. If I tried something and it disappointed me, that experience matters. But I want to remember what I actually tested. A conclusion from that attempt has a scope.

The same applies to a good experience. I can be excited by a result without assuming every task is now solved. What I take from him is a willingness to return to the work and look again.

---

## [SLIDE: Two: decide what is worth building — 02-selection-v1.png]

Second, being able to implement something makes product judgment more important.

DHH emphasizes decisions about the people software serves, its behavior, and its priorities when agents handle implementation.

That connects directly with what I care about. Imagine a small improvement to a business workflow. Before we build anything, someone has to notice the friction. Someone has to understand why the same information gets entered twice, or why a perfectly functional screen leaves people confused. 

If AI gives me more room to explore an idea, I want to use that room to understand the person who might use it. Producing more options only helps if we can choose something useful.

---

## [SLIDE: Three: keep the joy of creating — 00-cover.png]

Third, the joy of creating can survive a change in how we work.

When Lex asks about programmers' anxiety, DHH distinguishes enthusiasm for building things from attachment to programming's mechanical tasks. He also acknowledges uncertainty about employment. I hear an optimistic view of creating, without treating it as a promise about anyone's job.

My own excitement comes from seeing an idea become useful. Somebody can get on with the part of their day they actually care about.

I also admire the ambition behind Omarchy: taking strong preferences about a working environment and building a whole project around them. That impulse feels familiar to me. I want more worthwhile ideas to get a chance to exist.

---

## [SLIDE: What that means in my own projects — 01-first-version-v1.png]

Those three ideas come together in my blog workflow.

An idea can move through research, an outline, a draft, bilingual adaptation, and review. Each step has reusable guidance, and there are files I can inspect and revise. 

This piece is an example. I wanted to express why I agreed with DHH. An earlier draft gave too much weight to my interest in Linux. I asked for the emphasis to move back to AI, and to the different levels of trust in my own team.

The draft helped make that mismatch visible. I could point to what felt wrong and explain what mattered more. That is a kind of collaboration I value: moving an idea forward while I keep shaping its meaning.

---

## [SLIDE: Individual enthusiasm becomes a team question — 04-dhh-portrait-v1.png]

Inside a company, there is another challenge. My confidence does not automatically become somebody else's confidence.

I have colleagues who are actively using AI, and others who don't yet trust it enough. I don't want to turn DHH's enthusiasm into an argument that they should simply agree with me. His experience gives us a useful reference. Our project still needs its own evidence.

I would like the people using AI successfully to make their methods easier to inspect. And I want reservations to become specific questions we can investigate. 

---

## [SLIDE: Try one familiar task — 01-first-version-v1.png]

Here is a small exercise I would like us to try.

Take a paragraph based on an interview. Ask AI to make it clearer while preserving the meaning. Before we start, agree that a useful result must be readable, match the source, and keep personal interpretations separate from factual claims.

The person using AI can show the instructions, the source material, and the changes they accepted. A colleague can then examine the result against those same expectations.

That makes the discussion concrete. We can ask which edit improved the paragraph, which one needs checking, and whether the method is worth repeating.

---

## [SLIDE: Inspect the claim together — 02-selection-v1.png]

Consider a possible mistake in that exercise.

DHH describes agents doing useful work. That is a statement about the interview. More of my ideas may now be worth trying. That is my interpretation. Both can belong in a draft.

But if a rewrite turns them into everyone is more productive now, it has added a claim we haven't established. A confident sentence can sound better while becoming less accurate.

The enthusiastic person can show where the tool helped. The person with reservations can point to the claim that drifted. If the paragraph improved, keep the method. If it failed, adjust the instructions or review step. This is a practice I want to try, rather than a story about a team problem we have already solved.

---

## [SLIDE: The standard follows the consequences — 03-maintenance-v1.png]

A writing exercise would teach us about that task. Moving into a shared business system raises different questions.

I have written before about QA pressure when changes arrive faster than people can understand and verify them. More output can move the work into someone else's queue. That is part of delivery, too.

I still want to delegate more to AI. I also want to know who depends on the result, how we will notice a failure, and how we can recover. Trust can grow as we gain experience. The evidence we ask for should fit the consequences of getting something wrong.

---

## [SLIDE: Give enthusiasm somewhere useful to go — 00-cover.png]

For my team, I would like that enthusiasm to lead to a shared piece of work. We don't all have to start with the same confidence. We can start with a familiar task, agree on what good looks like, and inspect the result together.

If we finish with one method worth keeping, we have something we can use the next morning.

---

## [SLIDE: Keep the joy of creating — 00-cover.png]

So what I take from DHH is this: stay curious as the tools change, be thoughtful about what we build, and keep the joy of creating. That is why I share his enthusiasm for AI. I hope we can turn that enthusiasm into trust we earn together, and products that make someone's day a little better.
