On 5 July 2015, Greece held a referendum in the middle of a financial emergency.

The result was not close. **61.31%** voted No. **38.69%** voted Yes. [The official tally is still available from Greece's Interior Ministry](https://www.ypes.gr/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/ApotelDHMOPSIFISMA2015-20240129.pdf).

Then came the apparent reversal.

Yanis Varoufakis resigned the next day. Within days, Prime Minister Alexis Tsipras returned to negotiations. On 12–13 July, euro-area leaders agreed in principle to begin a new programme negotiation. In August, Greece entered a third assistance programme with an envelope of up to €86 billion. [The Council's programme record is here](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/policies/financial-assistance-eurozone-members/greece-programme/).

People said No. The government signed a new agreement anyway.

If you stop there, the story looks like either a betrayal or proof that democracy is theatre. Neither conclusion survives a closer look.

The arithmetic was clear. The mandate's scope was not.

## What did the ballot actually ask?

The referendum did not ask, “Do you reject every future bailout forever?”

It asked whether voters accepted two creditor documents submitted to the Eurogroup on 25 June 2015. [The Greek Interior Ministry published the wording and context](https://www.ypes.gr/gia-to-psifodeltio/). By the time Greeks voted, the immediate programme context had already moved; negotiations, deadlines, and available documents were changing underneath the question.

That does not make the vote empty. A 61% result is a huge political fact. It told Europe, creditors, and Tsipras's own government that the existing terms had lost democratic consent.

But a mandate is not a blank cheque written by reality.

It can tell a government what it should try to achieve. It cannot by itself create a safe path to every preferred outcome.

## The ability to walk away was shrinking

By late June, the negotiation was not taking place in an ordinary conference room.

Deposits were leaving Greek banks. The country faced intense liquidity pressure. On 28 June, following the referendum decision and the non-extension of the adjustment programme, the ECB held emergency liquidity assistance for Greek banks at the level set two days earlier. [The ECB's statement](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2015/html/pr150628.en.html) says so directly. Its annual report later described a bank holiday introduced in the context of widespread liquidity outflows.

This is why “just walk away” is not a complete political argument.

Greece had the formal right to reject a proposal. But a Grexit or disorderly break could have brought bank distress, capital flight, redenomination problems, import-price shocks, and chaos around contracts and savings. The exact counterfactual is unknowable. The risk was not.

In negotiation, a stated position only carries weight when the other side believes you can live with the alternative.

Greece could say No. Europe could also see that Greece feared the consequences of a break more immediately than the rest of the Eurozone did. The dependence was mutual, but it was not symmetrical.

That is painful to say because it sounds like an argument against democracy. The distinction is narrower: democratic legitimacy and economic optionality are different resources.

## A vote can change the table without changing every option

Tsipras had been elected in January on a promise to end austerity. The referendum deepened his mandate to reject the then-current creditor proposal. That mattered.

It changed the political posture of the negotiation. It increased the legitimacy cost of simply accepting the old terms. It made a new mandate necessary once the situation changed. It also showed Greek voters a conflict that normal elections often hide: the choice was not between a good agreement and a bad agreement; it was between several bad, risky, and politically costly paths.

This is why the constitutional and democratic argument remains real. Critics argued that accepting a new assistance framework violated the substance of the No vote. Others stressed that the ballot addressed specific documents rather than every future agreement. There is no reason to pretend the controversy disappears because the next deal had different paperwork.

Calling the third programme automatically void because a referendum had occurred would overstate the law. The political wound was deeper than a footnote; the legal conclusion remained contested.

Tsipras's response was consequential. He resigned in August and called a new election. In September, SYRIZA returned as the largest party with 35.46% of the vote and 145 seats. [The Hellenic Parliament's result page](https://www.hellenicparliament.gr/en/Vouli-ton-Ellinon/To-Politevma/Ekloges/Eklogika-apotelesmata-New/) records it.

That election does not mean “Greek people approved every line of the programme.” It does mean the public was asked again after the compromise had become visible—and chose to return Tsipras's party as the largest one.

The people did not speak one simple sentence three times. In January, July, and September, they answered different questions under radically different constraints.

## Authority is not capability

This is the part I keep thinking about when people talk about agent autonomy.

An organisation says an agent is authorised to issue refunds up to a limit. That is a mandate. But can it distinguish fraud from a frustrated customer? Can it see that a downstream system is stale? Can it stop when it reaches a policy conflict? Does it have a credible escalation path? Who owns the outcome if it makes the wrong call at scale?

“The agent was allowed to do it” does not answer any of those questions.

Neither does “the model is capable.” A model may be technically able to call a tool while lacking the context, data quality, permission boundary, or reversibility needed to use the tool safely.

For consequential delegation, I want five fields visible:

1. **Mandate:** what outcome has been legitimately requested?
2. **Permission:** which actions are explicitly allowed?
3. **Capability:** what can the actor reliably understand and execute?
4. **Constraint:** what conditions limit, pause, or block the action?
5. **Accountability:** who explains and repairs the result?

This is not a bureaucratic way to make every decision slow. It is how a system notices the difference between an instruction and an executable decision.

Low-risk work can be delegated quickly. A draft reply, a search, or a reversible update needs much less ceremony than a payment, a production deployment, or a decision that affects someone's rights. But the high-consequence cases need more than a checkbox saying “approved.”

They need a real option set, a bounded authority, and a person or institution that still owns the result.

## No was not meaningless

The lesson from Greece is not that economics beats democracy, or that votes are naïve because markets are powerful.

The lesson is harder: democracy gives a government legitimacy. It does not manufacture leverage, liquidity, or a safe exit where none exists. Those resources shape what a mandate can accomplish.

That does not make the mandate decorative. It tells us who has the right to negotiate, what compromises must be explained, and when a leader has to return to the public for renewed authority.

Before we give an agent the right to act, we should ask the same disciplined questions.

What did we actually authorise? What can it really do? What happens if its preferred path is not viable? And who remains accountable when the system has to choose an ugly compromise?
