# Editorial Brief

## Reader Pain

Delegators often assume a clear instruction settles a decision. In real systems, an authorization may conflict with the capability, constraints, and alternatives available to the actor.

## Reader Job To Be Done

Separate a mandate from permission, capability, and constraint before asking a human or agent to make a consequential decision.

## One-Sentence Promise

By the end, the reader can see why a 61.31% referendum result created real legitimacy without mechanically deciding every later negotiation outcome.

## Sharp Thesis

Authority is not capability. A mandate matters most when it changes what an actor can credibly do; it does not make an impossible or ruinous alternative suddenly viable.

## Concrete Opening

On 5 July 2015, 61.31% of Greek referendum voters chose “No.” Days later, the government returned to negotiations and accepted a new assistance framework. The arithmetic was clear. The mandate's scope was not.

## Counterargument

If economic constraints always trump votes, democracy becomes decorative. The answer is not to dismiss the vote: it can determine legitimacy, leadership, negotiating posture, and the need to seek a renewed mandate.

## Reusable Frame

**Mandate → permission → capability → constraint → accountability.** A decision is trustworthy only when all five are explicit.

## Distribution Hook

“A democratic mandate can change the bargaining table without changing the alternatives available underneath it.”

## Success Hypothesis

The referendum paradox should invite thoughtful disagreement from builders and operators who have seen approval processes confuse "allowed" with "possible." It applies the concrete-bottleneck growth lesson.
