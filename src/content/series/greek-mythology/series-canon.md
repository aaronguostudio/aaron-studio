# Greek Mythology Series Canon

## Scope and Delivery State

Season 1 is **Athens: Gods in Stone / 雅典：石中众神** (`greek-mythology-athens`). Its six available chapters enter myth through surviving architecture, sculpture, inscriptions, ancient texts, and later acts of reconstruction.

`available` means that a bilingual content package, interaction contract, registered source set, and core entity set exist and have passed package QA. It does not by itself mean that a chapter has been publicly published.

## Canon Principle

Greek mythology has no single official continuity. This series preserves identifiable traditions instead of silently merging them into one definitive plot.

- Name the ancient work, surviving object, site record, or scholarship behind a claim.
- When sources differ, keep their versions and dates or contexts visible.
- Do not turn a museum or scholarly reconstruction into direct evidence of a complete ancient scene.
- Do not treat an ancient narrative as proof that a supernatural or historical event occurred.
- Keep cult titles, disputed identities, later copies, and modern interpretations distinct from the beings or originals they concern.
- Prefer an honest gap over an invented bridge.

## Reconstruction Status

These four IDs are the only allowed values across articles, interactions, entity records, and image manifests.

| ID | Meaning | Reader-facing use |
|---|---|---|
| `artifact-documented` | Anchored in a surviving object, site, inscription, or institutional catalogue record. | Identify the surviving evidence and its date or inventory context. |
| `text-attested` | Attested in a named ancient text. It describes a tradition, not a verified historical event. | Name the author, work, book, and passage where useful. |
| `scholarly-reconstruction` | Reconstructed through published archaeological, philological, or art-historical analysis. | State what the inference uses and keep uncertainty visible. |
| `artistic-interpretation` | A creative choice that completes an image or experience without evidentiary force. | Label it plainly; never style it as documentary proof. |

Confidence may change inside one object or story. An overall subject can be well supported while an individual figure, gesture, room, date-specific appearance, or ritual moment remains uncertain.

## Cross-Chapter Identity Locks

1. **Athena Polias is not Athena Parthenos.** `cult-aspect-athena-polias` records Athena's civic cult aspect and the context of a lost ancient wooden image. `artwork-athena-parthenos` records the lost colossal gold-and-ivory image in the Parthenon. They concern Athena in different cult, material, and architectural settings.
2. **Poseidon-Erechtheus is a cult title, not a solved identity equation.** `cult-title-poseidon-erechtheus` records a source-backed linkage. It must not be used as an alias that proves Poseidon and Erechtheus were identical in every period or tradition.
3. **Erechtheus and Erichthonius remain version-sensitive.** Homer calls the earth-born figure fostered by Athena Erechtheus. Pseudo-Apollodorus later tells an Erichthonius story and then distinguishes a later Erechtheus in genealogy. The later distinction must not be projected backward into every earlier tradition.
4. **Korai is the documentary name; Caryatid is the familiar later label.** The Erechtheion figures' identities as libation bearers, dancers, captive women, or named mythic daughters remain interpretations. The figures standing on the monument today are protective copies; the originals are preserved elsewhere.
5. **A later copy is evidence, not duplication.** Roman reductions, component copies, early drawings, museum reassemblies, and modern protective replicas preserve selected information. None is registered as an exact substitute for a lost original or ancient viewing condition.
6. **Alternative scholarly identifications stay alternatives.** Hera/Eileithyia on the east pediment and the Erechtheid-sacrifice reading of the frieze are registered as competing or named proposals, not resolved identities.

## Chapter 1 Canon Lock — Athena and Poseidon

Locked claims:

1. The Parthenon west pediment is identified with the dispute between Athena and Poseidon. Use `pausanias-1.24.5` and the Acropolis Museum pediment records.
2. Only fragments of the central Athena and Poseidon figures survive. A reconstructed complete body, face, gesture, equipment set, surface, or color is not an extant statue.
3. Herodotus 8.55 names an olive tree and a “sea” jointly as Athenian testimonies; the sentence does not by itself assign each token to one deity. Pausanias 1.26.5 explicitly describes sea water and a trident mark as evidence for Poseidon's claim.
4. The 1674 drawing and surviving pieces support scholarly reconstruction of the composition but do not establish every original detail.
5. Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.14.1 supplies one later ancient version of the gifts, arbiters, Cecrops's testimony, and Athena's verdict. Its sequence is not the single original script.
6. The extant Erechtheion dates to 421–406 BCE. Institutional records support reconstructed cult associations and incorporated contest tokens; they do not prove equal divine status or a modern political program of reconciliation.
7. Herodotus reports that Athenians sent to sacrifice said a fresh shoot rose from the burned olive trunk. Reading that shoot as civic continuity is the chapter's editorial judgment, not Herodotus's stated meaning.
8. Do not silently equate Herodotus's earlier “temple of Erechtheus” with the extant late-fifth-century Erechtheion.

Not locked: one definitive judging procedure, exhaustive pediment cast, exact contest sequence, original color scheme, or generated face, pose, weather, and lighting.

## Chapter 2 Canon Lock — Athena's Birth and the East Pediment

Locked claims:

1. Pausanias and the Acropolis Museum identify Athena's birth from Zeus as the subject of the Parthenon east pediment.
2. The central birth action is lost. The surviving marginal sculptures and fragments, dated around 437–432 BCE in their object records, preserve witnesses and temporal framing more securely than the missing center.
3. Helios rising and Selene descending support the institutional reconstruction of dawn. That framing is a scholarly reading of surviving figures, not a carved caption.
4. The large near-central female figure may be identified as Hera or Eileithyia. The registry deliberately records both candidates without selecting one.
5. Hesiod's swallowing of Metis and head-birth, the Homeric Hymn's cosmic reaction, and Pindar's axe-assisted version are distinct textual witnesses. They may be compared but not fused into a single ancient screenplay or presented as a direct scene-by-scene key to the sculpture.

The chapter's judgment that the stone now preserves witnesses more fully than the birth action is an editorial reading. A complete Zeus, Athena, central pose, cast, attachment system, face, color distribution, and exact birth moment remain scholarly or artistic reconstruction.

## Chapter 3 Canon Lock — The Erechtheion's Many Memories

Locked claims:

1. The extant Erechtheion dates to 421–406 BCE. Its uneven levels and irregular plan are materially visible; explaining that form through terrain and multiple cult requirements is an institutional and scholarly reconstruction.
2. Athena Polias belongs to the civic cult and lost wooden-image tradition of the northern Acropolis sanctuary. She must not be conflated with Athena Parthenos.
3. Pausanias records an altar of Poseidon where sacrifice was also offered to Erechtheus, plus sea water and a trident outline shown as testimony for Poseidon's claim. The compound Poseidon-Erechtheus tradition links the names but does not settle their ontological relationship.
4. Homer 2.546–551 and Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.14.6, 3.14.8, and 3.15.1 preserve different arrangements of Erechtheus, Erichthonius, Athena, Pandrosus, and Boutes. No single genealogy retroactively corrects all others.
5. Pausanias places the Pandroseion beside Athena's sanctuary and associates the olive with Athena's land claim. The fragmentary building account IG I³ 474 mentions the ancient image, “maidens,” and work facing the Kekropion and Pandroseion; mapping every term onto a surviving room or wall remains interpretive.
6. The six female supports are documented as Korai in the accounts. Five originals are preserved in Athens and one in London, while protective copies stand on the monument. Display locations may change; their mythic or ritual identities are not settled.
7. The olive, sea water, trident mark, neighboring sanctuaries, cult associations, and architectural phases do not by themselves produce a complete original floor plan or one unified ancient story.

The chapter's judgment that the building preserves accumulated, not neatly reconciled, memories is editorial and open to challenge. Exact interior arrangement, equal cult status, Korai identity, complete olive history, and a modern political allegory are not locked.

## Chapter 4 Canon Lock — Athena Parthenos

Locked claims:

1. Athena Parthenos was a colossal gold-and-ivory image made under Pheidias's direction for the Parthenon; the original is lost. Ancient description and later evidence support an approximate height of twelve metres and major attributes, not an exact full appearance.
2. The 439/8 BCE public account EM 637 is evidence for work and materials associated with the statue, not a complete workshop diary.
3. Thucydides 2.13.5 reports Pericles describing forty talents of gold as removable and repayable in extremity. The passage does not prove the gold was actually removed, does not require a modern-weight conversion, and does not reduce the statue to a “bank.”
4. Pausanias is a later witness to the statue's attributes. His identification of the serpent as Erichthonius is source-specific, not an inscription surviving from the original commission.
5. The Varvakeion Athena, Acropolis copy Acr. 1362, and Strangford Shield preserve selected features through later reduction or copying. They are not exact duplicates, and proposed portraits on the shield remain proposed.
6. The original's precise disappearance and final fate are unknown.

The chapter's judgment that a distributed archive restores an outline rather than the original encounter is editorial. Exact face, proportions, support structure, surface, color, lighting, full accessory arrangement, and a definitive destruction narrative are not locked.

## Chapter 5 Canon Lock — The Parthenon Frieze

Locked claims:

1. The continuous Ionic frieze ran around the upper exterior of the Parthenon's cella. Institutional records describe 115 blocks, about 160 metres, carved around 443–438 BCE, with two streams converging on the east.
2. Surviving blocks, fragments, early drawings, and modern digital reassembly make the sequence more continuous for us, but they belong to different dates and locations.
3. No surviving ancient commentary supplies the frieze's title. The Greater Panathenaia is the strongest broad-consensus interpretation of the program, not a caption carved in stone.
4. Plato attests a robe carried to the Acropolis at the Greater Panathenaia. He does not mention the frieze or identify its east-center textile.
5. Five figures, head-borne objects, and a folded textile are materially documented at the east center. The participants, direction of transfer, and ritual moment remain disputed.
6. Joan Breton Connelly's Erechtheid-sacrifice proposal is a serious named scholarly alternative. It is neither a recovered ancient title nor equal in consensus weight to the Panathenaic reading.

The chapter's contrast between mortal motion and divine stillness is an editorial visual judgment, not a recovered Athenian slogan. Exact identities, social representativeness, political ideology, color, sound, continuous motion, and completed gaps are not locked.

## Chapter 6 Canon Lock — The Theatre of Dionysus

Locked claims:

1. The south-slope sanctuary and theatre developed through multiple phases. The fifth-century performance space used earth and timber; most visible monumental stone belongs to rebuilding around 350–320 BCE, with later Hellenistic, Roman, and late-antique changes.
2. Postholes and foundations support an early venue but not a complete seating plan, façade, capacity, or sightline map for a 458 BCE premiere.
3. Drama belonged within the City Dionysia's religious, civic, and competitive setting. A fourth-century dithyrambic victory dedication demonstrates organized, sponsored competition but is not a fifth-century tragedy program or full festival schedule.
4. Aristotle's account of tragedy developing from dithyrambic leaders is later ancient testimony. Modern scholarship treats the origins as sparse and disputed; a single linear ritual-to-tragedy story is not locked.
5. The *Oresteia* was produced at the City Dionysia in 458 BCE. In *Eumenides* 674–710, Athena calls jurors to vote and announces a court for Attica within the play.
6. The dramatic court does not prove that Athena founded a historical Athenian institution, that the spectators became jurors, or that the play means only “democracy replaces revenge.”
7. Aristotle's example of Agathon's lost *Antheus* establishes that tragedy was not formally confined to inherited myth; it does not quantify how common invented plots were.
8. The transmitted plays contain few possible stage directions. Exact blocking, masks, costume, music, chorus formation, voices, daylight, crowd composition, and response remain reconstruction or art.
9. The Met actor statuette is evidence for broader performance culture, not an actor, costume, or production record from *Eumenides* in 458 BCE.

“Myth became a public argument” is the chapter's bounded editorial judgment: competitive shared performance gave inherited conflicts voices, sequence, duration, and public comparison. It does not imply equal access, direct political participation, a single audience verdict, or a complete recovery of the premiere.

## Naming Policy

- Public prose uses the preferred EN/ZH names in `entity-bible.json`.
- Stable IDs, not translated strings, connect articles, interactions, images, and analytics.
- Ancient Greek forms are reference data, not mandatory display copy.
- On first mention, add a clarifying form only when it helps the reader; do not overload the story with transliteration.
- Use `Parthenon` / `帕特农神庙`, not “Pantheon” or “阿佛农神庙”.
- Keep Athena Polias / 雅典娜·波利阿斯 and Athena Parthenos / 雅典娜·帕耳忒诺斯 distinct.
- Use Erechtheus / 厄瑞克忒斯 and Erichthonius / 厄里克托尼俄斯 according to the cited version; do not normalize one into the other.

## Source Policy

Use `source-registry.json` as the shared source index. A chapter claim ledger may narrow a source's use but may not expand it beyond the registry's `supports` and `limitations` without review.

Priority for this season:

1. Institutional object, inscription, and site records.
2. Identifiable ancient passages, presented as traditions or dramatic texts.
3. Peer-reviewed or museum-backed reconstruction research.
4. Responsible secondary explanation when the first three do not answer the reader's question.

Source images, translations, figures, museum renders, and digital-tour captures require their own rights review. A public URL does not grant reuse rights.

## Continuity Change Protocol

1. Update the owning registry.
2. Increment its semantic version.
3. Add a dated revision entry.
4. Find every chapter, interaction, prompt, and accepted asset using the changed ID.
5. Mark affected downstream material stale until reviewed.

Stable IDs must not be renamed after publication. Deprecate an ID with a replacement pointer instead. Disputed identities should be represented with qualified relationships or interpretation entities rather than silently changing a preferred name.
