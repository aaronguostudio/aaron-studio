# Editorial Brief

## Primary reader

A curious non-specialist, family traveler, or mythology reader who recognizes Athena's head-birth but does not yet know how that story relates to the Parthenon east pediment or why modern reconstructions disagree.

## Reader pain

Popular retellings merge Hesiod, a Homeric Hymn, Pindar, and later mythography into one cinematic sequence. Museum displays then appear to confirm that sequence, even though the central statues are lost and several surviving figures remain disputed.

## Reader job and promise

**Job:** understand what the east pediment securely tells us, how three ancient poetic traditions differ, and what a responsible visual reconstruction may add.

**Promise:** after reading, the reader will be able to distinguish the shared core—Athena born from Zeus's head—from the variant details of Metis, the axe, the armament, the cosmic response, and the exact sculptural cast.

## Sharp thesis

The Parthenon staged Athena's birth as a cosmic interruption framed by dawn; because its center is lost, the surviving witnesses reveal the event's scale more securely than its exact choreography.

## Concrete opening

At the east pediment, the eye is pulled toward an empty center. At one corner the sun rises; at the other the moon descends. The birth is missing, but its time and audience still point toward it.

## Original contribution

Instead of using the pediment as an illustration for a preassembled myth, the chapter reads inward from the surviving edges. It places Hesiod, Homeric Hymn 28, and Pindar beside the stones, showing that each supplies a different part of the familiar scene.

## Why Aaron can write this

Aaron's authority is that of an attentive visitor who can turn a difficult evidence boundary into an inviting reading experience. He is not claiming to resolve disputed sculpture identifications or the textual history of Athena's birth.

## Authority and scope boundary

- The pediment and ancient poems attest mythic traditions, not a supernatural or historical event.
- Pausanias and the Acropolis Museum securely identify the subject as Athena's birth from Zeus's head.
- The surviving center does not establish an exact instant, pose, cast list, or whether an axe was visibly included.
- Hesiod includes Metis and the head-birth but does not name Hephaestus or an axe in the core account used here.
- Pindar explicitly includes Hephaestus and the bronze axe; this is one ancient poetic version, not a universal script.
- Homeric Hymn 28 supplies the armed emergence and cosmic reaction; its imagery must not be attributed automatically to the sculptors.
- Exact identifications of several peripheral figures remain debated.
- Museum statements that Helios and Selene indicate dawn are institutional reconstruction; the surviving objects themselves are artifact evidence.

## Evidence needed

- Acropolis Museum overview and east-pediment object records for subject, date, material, surviving edge figures, missing center, and uncertainty.
- Pausanias 1.24.5 for the ancient identification of the front pediment.
- Hesiod, *Theogony* 886–900 and 924–929 for Metis, succession anxiety, and Zeus giving birth from his head.
- Homeric Hymn 28 for armed Athena, the watching gods, cosmic disturbance, and Helios stopping his horses.
- Pindar, *Olympian* 7.35–39 for Hephaestus's axe and Athena's cry.
- British Museum object record and Dyfri Williams's 2013 study for the limits and continuing revision of figure identification and placement.

## Credible counterargument

The sunrise-to-moonset frame does not necessarily encode the full metaphysical meaning of the birth; it may primarily establish time and fill the pediment's corners. The article should call “cosmic interruption” a reading supported by the visual frame and Hymn 28, not an inscription left by the sculptors.

## Reusable reader lens

When a famous myth seems to match a monument perfectly, ask:

1. Which part is named by an ancient observer?
2. Which part physically survives?
3. Which detail comes from which text?
4. Which connecting action has been reconstructed?

## Interaction hypothesis

A dawn-to-center evidence map can let the reader reveal Helios, Selene, named or disputed witnesses, and the absent Zeus–Athena center. Selecting “text” should show how Hesiod, Hymn 28, and Pindar add different details without visually fusing them into one authoritative reconstruction.

## Kill criteria

Stop or reframe the chapter if it:

- presents Metis, Hephaestus, the axe, and the Hymn's cosmic reaction as one original account;
- labels every surviving figure with certainty;
- presents a complete generated center as documentary reconstruction;
- claims the pediment directly illustrates one surviving poem;
- turns Athena's birth into a modern business or psychology allegory.
