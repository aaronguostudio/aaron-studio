# Athena and Poseidon: Who Should Guard Athens?

On this family trip through Greece, the Acropolis drew me most strongly toward what was missing.

Standing before ruins, the mind starts repairing what the eye cannot see. A roof returns above the columns, broken edges straighten, and the empty triangle of the west pediment fills with gods. That kind of imagining comes almost automatically.

But the original scene is not waiting beneath the dust, intact. Athena survives in pieces of a head and neck, a shoulder, part of her aegis, a calf, and part of a foot. Poseidon, too, remains incomplete. The two figures at the center of the story are now among its greatest absences.

The gap nevertheless has a name.

When Pausanias visited Athens in the second century CE, he identified the rear pediment of the Parthenon as the contest between Athena and Poseidon for the land. The Acropolis Museum identifies the same subject today. The stone no longer gives us a complete picture, but the ancient description tells us what kind of drama once occupied it.

## Two witnesses on the rock

Centuries before Pausanias, Herodotus recorded an Athenian tradition. On the Acropolis, he wrote, an olive tree and a “sea” were shown as witnesses left by Athena and Poseidon when they contended for the land.

Pausanias later recorded Poseidon's claim in more physical detail. He describes sea water in a cistern inside the Erechtheion; according to the account, a south wind made it sound like waves. On the rock was the outline of a trident. The legend presented both as evidence for Poseidon's claim.

These passages give us a dispute, two gods, an olive tree, salt water, and the mark of a trident. They do not give us one seamless ancient screenplay. They do not preserve every action, name every witness, or tell us exactly what each figure looked like high above the temple.

The familiar story grows between those surviving points.

## One version fills in the action

In one later ancient account, the *Library* attributed to Pseudo-Apollodorus, Poseidon arrives in Attica first. He strikes the middle of the Acropolis with his trident and produces a sea. Athena comes after him, calls Cecrops as witness, and plants an olive tree.

The two gods then contend for the land. In this version, Zeus separates them and appoints the twelve gods as arbiters. Cecrops testifies that Athena was the first to plant the olive, and the country is awarded to her.

That account is useful because it gives the story movement: arrival, signs, dispute, judgment. It also warns us against treating its own sequence as the only canon. The same passage preserves competing claims that other arbiters decided the contest.

So I do not need to invent a definitive vote, or make every ancient author agree on the rules. I can tell this version while keeping its edges visible.

Athena wins. That should not be blurred. If the story ended there, it would be clean: one winner, one loser; one gift accepted and another refused. The Acropolis preserves a more interesting ending.

## Athena wins—and Poseidon stays

Walk away from the Parthenon toward the Erechtheion and Poseidon returns.

The extant Erechtheion, built between 421 and 406 BCE, was not a simple temple for one god. Museum and conservation records describe an eastern room dedicated to Athena and a lower western room with shrines including Poseidon-Erechtheus. The complex also incorporated sacred signs associated with the contest: the olive, trident marks, and the salt-water spring.

In Pseudo-Apollodorus's version, the verdict gives the land to Athena. The cult layout and sacred signs recorded at the Erechtheion show that Poseidon's claim still occupied the sacred landscape. I read that juxtaposition as victory without erasure.

The olive and salt water were not discarded stage props after the contest. They remained in the sacred landscape Athenians continued to visit and describe, like two testimonies from an argument the city kept visible. Athena remained the victorious guardian in the story; Poseidon remained in its rituals, architecture, and memory.

This does not make the result a tie. Athena's place is unmistakable. Nor does a sanctuary shared among several cults prove a modern program of tolerance or political reconciliation. The narrower observation is strong enough: victory did not require erasure.

## What the city chose to remember

It is tempting to turn the gifts into a tidy allegory. The olive becomes agriculture and settled order; salt water becomes seafaring, danger, or power. Athena becomes reason, Poseidon untamed nature. Those readings may be interesting, but the sources used here do not hand us that single interpretation.

What they do show is a city keeping two powers in view.

Herodotus gives the olive tree another life. After the Persians burned the sanctuary on the Acropolis, he says, Athenians who went up to sacrifice reported that a fresh shoot had already risen from the damaged trunk. This is not proof of a supernatural event. It is evidence of how Athenians told the story of their own continuity.

A sanctuary could burn, yet the tree could still become a sign that the city's life had not ended. In this chapter's reading of the Athenian story, the olive answers more than the question of who won: it also asks how a damaged city might imagine that it would continue.

The myth is richer when we resist choosing one final moral. The olive can carry Athena's victory and the city's recovery. The salt water can carry Poseidon's failed claim and his continuing presence. The Acropolis holds both, without pretending the conflict never happened.

## The distance between evidence and imagination

What remains for us now are fragments, ancient texts, and later acts of reconstruction. A drawing made in 1674 preserves some information about the pediment before the explosion of 1687, and surviving sculptures allow scholars to reason about placement and movement. None of these sources can return every face, gesture, object, or color with certainty.

It lets us feel the distance between what survives and what imagination must supply.

That distance is one reason I want to turn this trip into a series and keep telling these stories.

The question on the west pediment sounds simple: Athena or Poseidon—who should guard Athens?

The familiar answer is Athena. But the sacred landscape preserves a second answer: a city may choose one power without forgetting the other.

Myth gave Athena the victory. Athens still gave Poseidon a place.

That is not a loose end. It is how the city made the conflict part of its memory.
