# Editorial Brief

## Primary reader

A curious non-specialist who has entered the Parthenon ruins, seen a modern reconstruction, or encountered the Varvakeion Athena and wants to know what can actually be recovered about Pheidias's lost statue.

## Reader pain

Search results often move seamlessly from ancient descriptions to Roman miniatures and modern full-scale recreations. The reader is shown a complete image without being told which details are directly attested, copied centuries later, inferred by scholars, or invented by a modern artist.

## Reader job and promise

**Job:** understand what Athena Parthenos was, why its materials mattered to Athens, and how several imperfect witnesses allow—but limit—reconstruction.

**Promise:** after reading, the reader will recognize the statue's secure core features and will know why no modern version should be labeled an exact replica of Pheidias's lost work.

## Sharp thesis

Athena Parthenos survives as a distributed archive: ancient words, public accounts, reduced copies, and reproduced details recover the goddess's outline, but not the full artistic presence of the lost original.

## Concrete opening

Enter the Parthenon today and the place designed for its dominant figure is empty. Then Pausanias begins to restore her in words: gold and ivory, a Sphinx and griffins, a Nike tall enough to be a statue in its own right, spear, shield, serpent, and a base crowded with Pandora's birth.

## Original contribution

The article does not simply describe the statue. It lets each class of evidence add a layer and preserve its own limitation. The financial accounts and Thucydides reveal that the divine image was also made from removable public wealth; the Roman copies show how the image traveled beyond its original body; the gaps prevent a false “exact reconstruction.”

## Why Aaron can write this

Aaron can guide readers through a visual reconstruction as a transparent product of evidence and choice. His authority is editorial and experiential, not archaeological: he asks the visitor's question and keeps every answer tied to a source.

## Authority and scope boundary

- The original statue is lost; the chapter will not claim a known final destruction, transfer to Constantinople, or exact disappearance date.
- Pausanias describes the statue centuries after its fifth-century-BCE creation; he is a strong ancient witness, not a workshop record.
- Thucydides records Pericles's claim that forty talents of refined gold could be removed and later replaced. Do not convert this into exact kilograms or modern value without a separate metrological study.
- The Acropolis financial inscription documents state oversight and gold expenditure; it does not preserve the statue's complete construction manual.
- The Acropolis and Varvakeion statuettes are later Roman reductions. They preserve important types and features but not exact scale, surface, face, colour, or every accessory.
- The Strangford Shield is a Roman copy of the shield, not a surviving piece of the original. The museum's identification of Pheidias and Pericles among its figures should not be turned into certainty about the original portrait claim.
- A modern full-scale recreation may convey scale, but it is an artistic and scholarly interpretation with its own date and materials.

## Evidence needed

- Pausanias 1.24.5–7 for material, helmet, standing pose, Medusa, Nike, spear, shield, serpent, and Pandora base.
- Acropolis Museum financial account EM 637 for state oversight, annual accounting, and gold expenditure.
- Thucydides 2.13.5 for forty talents of removable refined gold and the obligation to replace it.
- National Archaeological Museum record for the twelve-metre original and Varvakeion Roman reduction.
- Acropolis Museum object Ακρ. 1362 for a second, fragmentary Roman replica and its preserved pose and dress.
- British Museum Strangford Shield for one later reproduction of a single richly decorated component.
- Peer-reviewed scholarship on reproduction and on the unavoidable uncertainty in reconstructing Pheidias's artistic form.

## Credible counterargument

The ancient descriptions and copies agree on enough features that excessive caution can become unhelpful. The article should not pretend that nothing is known. Its method is tiered confidence: the standing goddess, Nike, shield, spear, serpent, aegis, and major decorative program are strong; face, exact proportions, support system, colour distribution, fine composition, and overall artistic effect remain less secure.

## Reusable reader lens

To evaluate a reconstruction of a lost work, ask:

1. What did an ancient observer describe?
2. What did contemporary records count or pay for?
3. What do later copies repeat?
4. Where do those copies diverge or omit?
5. What has the modern maker chosen?

## Interaction hypothesis

An evidence-assembly interaction should begin with the empty naos and a neutral silhouette. Text, accounts, statuettes, and shield evidence add separate overlays. The final coloured reconstruction remains visibly labeled “artistic interpretation” and never earns a false 100% certainty state.

## Kill criteria

Stop or reframe the chapter if it:

- calls the Varvakeion Athena or Nashville Athena an exact copy;
- states a definitive story of the original's destruction without a dedicated late-antique evidence pass;
- presents forty talents as a modern cash-value comparison;
- treats later copies as passive photographs rather than objects with their own contexts;
- makes every decorative figure or colour certain;
- turns the statue into a business, treasury, or digital-twin metaphor.
