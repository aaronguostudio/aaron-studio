# Blog Plan: The Erechtheion — One Building, Many Memories

## Meta

- **Author:** Aaron Guo
- **Target:** Greek Mythology special on aaronguo.com (EN + ZH bilingual)
- **Tone:** Story-first, spatial, source-aware, warm, and quietly wondrous; no teacherly survey, modern political allegory, or forced business/AI analogy
- **Length:** 1,600–2,100 words EN; 2,600–3,800 Chinese characters
- **Audience:** Curious non-specialists, families, and Greece travelers who recognize the Caryatids but do not yet understand the building around them
- **CTA:** Continue to the previous or next chapter in *Athens: Gods in Stone / 雅典：石中众神*

## Hook

Open with a question the building itself creates: why does this temple refuse to give us one balanced front and one clean story? From the south, six women seem to explain the monument at a glance. Walk around it and that confidence disappears: the ground changes level, the entrances do not align, its sides refuse to match, and the image that once mattered most is gone. Within the first 150 words, turn “awkward design” into the chapter's governing question: what did this building have to keep?

## Thesis

The Erechtheion's asymmetry cannot be explained by myth or terrain alone: the surviving structure, ancient building accounts, and institutional reconstructions show a monument adapting to uneven ground while accommodating multiple cults and neighboring sacred places, making “many obligations preserved in one place” a defensible—but not exclusive—way to read its form.

## Personal Anchor

Aaron's family journey through Greece made the gods on the Acropolis feel embedded in actual buildings rather than isolated in a mythology book. Use only that supplied experience to motivate the question; do not invent a family conversation, exact route, weather, or individual reaction, and never use personal memory as historical evidence.

## Outline

### Part 1: A building that refuses a single front

- **Job:** Earn the title immediately by showing that the Erechtheion cannot be understood from the Caryatid porch alone.
- **Story beat:** Circle from the famous south porch toward the other façades; let changing levels and entrances dismantle the expectation of a symmetrical Greek temple.
- **Evidence:** Date the extant building to 421–406 BCE and introduce the official explanation that both uneven terrain and multiple cult requirements shaped it (`ysma-erechtheion`; `acropolis-museum-erechtheion`).
- **Judgment:** State the article's wager early: the irregularity may be evidence of what the place had to preserve, not a flaw to explain away.
- **Boundary and transition:** Do not assign every wall to a myth. Ask what occupied the building before moving inside.

### Part 2: The Athena at the center who is no longer visible

- **Job:** Replace the reader's likely mental image of Athena Parthenos with Athena Polias and the lost wooden cult image.
- **Story beat:** Enter through the east porch in the institutional reconstruction and encounter an absence: the city's old image mattered more ritually than the monument's most photographed figures, but it has not survived.
- **Evidence:** Use the 409/8 BCE building account's reference to the temple containing the ancient image, the Acropolis Museum's Athena Polias record, and Pausanias' later heaven-sent tradition (`attic-inscriptions-ig-i3-474`; `acropolis-museum-athena-polias`; `pausanias-1.26.6-7`, all proposed).
- **Boundary:** Explain Polias as an aspect/title of Athena, not a second goddess. Separate the documented cult setting from the unprovable divine origin and unrecoverable appearance of the xoanon.
- **Transition:** The eastern room does not exhaust the building; descend toward the stranger name in the west.

### Part 3: A hyphen in the lower room

- **Job:** Make Poseidon-Erechtheus understandable without falsely solving the relationship.
- **Story beat:** In the reconstructed lower western space, several names gather—Poseidon-Erechtheus, Hephaistos, and Boutes. Slow down on the hyphen instead of letting it pass as a label.
- **Evidence:** Pair Pausanias' Poseidon altar, where sacrifice was also offered to Erechtheus, with Pseudo-Apollodorus' priesthood of Athena and Poseidon-Erechtheus (`pausanias-1.26.5`; `pseudo-apollodorus-3.15.1`, proposed).
- **Judgment:** The safe conclusion is close cultic association, not a universal equation between the sea god and every story about the local king.
- **Transition:** If one hyphen can hold two identities in tension, the names Erechtheus and Erichthonius make the problem larger.

### Part 4: Two names, no universal family tree

- **Job:** Teach source variation through a short narrative comparison rather than a genealogy lecture.
- **Story beat:** Let the same cluster of traits travel between names: in Homer, earth-born Erechtheus is fostered by Athena; in Pseudo-Apollodorus, Erichthonius receives the earth-birth and Athena-upbringing story, while a later Erechtheus belongs to another generation.
- **Evidence:** Use `homer-iliad-2.546-551`, `pseudo-apollodorus-3.14.6`, `pseudo-apollodorus-3.14.8`, `pseudo-apollodorus-3.15.1`, and the two proposed OCD entries.
- **Reader tool:** Say “in this source” each time the names change. A three-step timeline is clearer and more honest than a single authoritative family tree.
- **Boundary:** Modern scholarship leaves their original relationship unresolved; do not merge them or declare one genealogy correct.
- **Transition:** Return from names to things a visitor could once be shown in and around the sanctuary.

### Part 5: The olive, the sea, and the marks in the rock

- **Job:** Reconnect Chapter 1's divine contest to physical tokens and neighboring sacred space without turning tradition into proof of miracle.
- **Story beat:** Move outside the main walls: an olive associated with Athena, sea water and a trident outline associated with Poseidon's claim, and the nearby Pandroseion create a constellation rather than one tidy interior exhibit.
- **Evidence:** Preserve the distinction between Herodotus' joint list of olive and “sea” and Pausanias' more explicit Poseidon attribution; connect the Pandrosium/Pandrosus traditions and the building account's spatial language (`herodotus-8.55`; `pausanias-1.26.5`; `pausanias-1.27.1-3`, proposed; `pseudo-apollodorus-3.14.1`; `attic-inscriptions-ig-i3-474`, proposed).
- **Boundary:** Do not claim that today's tree is the same biological individual, that the ancient water survives unchanged, or that the fragmentary sources yield an exact site plan.
- **Internal link:** Link naturally to Chapter 1 for the fuller contest narrative; do not retell the verdict here.
- **Transition:** The most famous surviving figures now return, but as a question rather than an answer.

### Part 6: Six women carry certainty and uncertainty together

- **Job:** Use the Caryatids to show how a secure object fact can coexist with an unsettled meaning.
- **Story beat:** Begin with weight: six sculpted maidens supported the south porch, and their hair and drapery participated in the structural solution. Then reveal that the on-site figures are copies, five originals are in Athens, and one is in London.
- **Evidence:** Ground the object, date, display history, ancient “maidens” vocabulary, and range of interpretations in the Acropolis Museum record and IG I³ 474; use Jütte only for the limited critique of Vitruvius' later punishment story (`acropolis-museum-erechtheion-kore-a`; `attic-inscriptions-ig-i3-474`; `jutte-2022-contested-caryatids`, all proposed).
- **Boundary:** Present punished women of Caryae, ritual participants, daughters of Kekrops, and libation bearers as interpretations. The Museum's preferred reading is persuasive institutional synthesis, not an ancient name tag.
- **Transition:** Pull back from one porch to all the building's unequal sides.

### Part 7: What asymmetry can remember

- **Job:** Resolve the opening question without pretending to resolve the monument.
- **Story beat:** Revisit the route around the building, now with the eastern image, western cults, two-name problem, contest tokens, neighboring sanctuaries, and Korai visible as separate evidence layers.
- **Judgment:** Offer Aaron's bounded reading: the Erechtheion preserves adjacency without producing one canonical story. It is not a monument to equality or reconciliation; it is a place where several sacred obligations remained difficult to simplify.
- **Counterweight:** Name terrain, later textual dates, and incomplete interior knowledge once more so the metaphor of memory does not outrun the evidence.
- **Ending image:** Leave the reader facing the Caryatids again, no longer mistaking the building's most famous feature for its whole meaning.
- **CTA:** Continue through the series hub and use previous/next navigation; link forward to the Athena Parthenos chapter only after it is live.
