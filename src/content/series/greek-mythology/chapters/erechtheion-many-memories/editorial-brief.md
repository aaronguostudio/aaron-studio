# Editorial Brief

## Primary reader

A curious non-specialist who has seen the Erechtheion or its Caryatids, knows the Athena-and-Poseidon contest, and wants to understand why this small, irregular monument mattered without needing a background in Greek religion or architectural history.

## Reader pain

Most visitor summaries offer a list—Athena, Poseidon, Erechtheus, an olive tree, a salt-water spring, Caryatids—but do not explain how those names relate or where certainty ends. The result is either confusion or a falsely tidy “one temple, one myth” story.

## Reader job and one-sentence promise

**Job:** learn to read the Erechtheion as a layered sacred complex while keeping cult, myth, architecture, and later reconstruction distinct.

**Promise:** after reading, the reader will recognize what each side of the building was understood to hold, why the plan is unusual, and which famous explanations remain uncertain.

## Sharp thesis

The Erechtheion's irregularity is not a defect to explain away. The surviving architecture and institutional reconstructions show a sanctuary adapting to uneven ground while accommodating several cults and sacred places; reading that accumulation as “memory made architectural” is the chapter's bounded interpretation.

## Concrete opening

Begin outside the south porch. Six women appear to hold the roof, and the visitor thinks they understand the building because they recognize its icon. Then move around the monument: no two sides behave alike, the floors do not share one level, and its most important cult image is gone. Recognition gives way to a better question—what demanded such an unusual form?

The opening may be framed by Aaron's family Acropolis trip but must not invent a family exchange or a precise observation he did not provide.

## Original contribution

The chapter will connect five things that are usually presented separately:

1. Athena Polias and the age-old wooden cult image, distinct from Athena Parthenos.
2. The western cult complex and the difficult formulation Poseidon-Erechtheus.
3. The changing relationship between Erechtheus and Erichthonius across sources.
4. The contest tokens and the neighboring Pandroseion, with exact-location and continuity limits.
5. The Korai/Caryatids as both documented architectural supports and disputed symbolic figures.

The contribution is not a new archaeological reconstruction. It is a readable map of evidence, naming, and uncertainty that turns the building's complexity into the story.

## Why Aaron can write this

Aaron's authority is the well-researched visitor's question, not classical expertise. The series began from a real family trip and a desire to make the gods in the Acropolis legible. His strength is designing a reading experience in which curiosity and source discipline reinforce one another.

## Authority and scope boundary

- The extant Erechtheion dates to 421–406 BCE; do not silently equate it with every earlier “temple of Erechtheus” or Athena sanctuary named in an older text.
- Institutional pages reconstruct the eastern and western cult spaces, but later damage and conversion mean the original interior is only partly known.
- Athena Polias means Athena in her civic/protective aspect. Her old wooden cult image is not Athena Parthenos.
- “Poseidon-Erechtheus” is a cultic formulation with a complex relationship to the mythic king. Do not write that the two were simply and universally the same character.
- Homeric Erechtheus carries traits later sources assign to Erichthonius; Pseudo-Apollodorus distinguishes them genealogically. Scholarship has not produced a universally accepted original relationship.
- The olive, salt water, and trident mark are attested as sacred testimonies in ancient texts and institutionally associated with the complex. Do not claim that a present tree is the original tree, that ancient water remains, or that every visible opening is an untouched divine mark.
- The building inscriptions call the six supports Korai. Their identification as libation bearers above the Kekropion is a scholarly interpretation, not a surviving ancient label.
- Vitruvius' punishment story for “Caryatids” is a later Roman explanation and is disputed; it cannot be presented as the figures' settled meaning.

## Evidence needed

- YSMA and Acropolis Museum descriptions of chronology, two levels, cult spaces, sacred tokens, later damage, and restoration.
- Acropolis Museum record for Athena Polias and the wooden cult image.
- Pausanias 1.26.5–1.27.3 for the altar, sea-water cistern, trident outline, sacred image, olive, Pandrosus, and ritual maidens.
- Herodotus 8.55 and Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.14.1 for the contest tokens, with the Chapter 1 boundaries preserved.
- Homer, *Iliad* 2.546–551; Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.14.6, 3.14.8, and 3.15.1; and Oxford Classical Dictionary entries for the Erechtheus/Erichthonius naming problem.
- Acropolis Museum Caryatid record and IG I³ 474 for the six Korai, Kekropion/Pandroseion adjacency, and building-account vocabulary.

## Credible counterarguments

### “The irregular plan is mostly a topographic engineering response.”

Yes. Uneven terrain is an explicit part of the institutional explanation. The article must not pretend that cult alone caused every wall and level. Its narrower claim is that terrain and several sacred obligations were accommodated together.

### “Calling the building an archive of memory is modern poetry.”

Yes. It is an editorial lens, not an ancient term or archaeological category. The article earns the lens by first documenting the coexistence of cult rooms, sacred tokens, adjacent shrines, and ancestor traditions.

### “Erechtheus and Erichthonius may originally have been the same figure.”

Possibly, but the scholarship is inconclusive and surviving sources do not behave uniformly. The chapter should show the divergence, not adjudicate an unknowable original form.

### “The Caryatids' identity has already been solved.”

The Acropolis Museum favors the libation-bearer reading while explicitly listing other interpretations. Public prose may call that reading persuasive, never certain.

## Reusable reader lens

To read a layered sanctuary, ask:

1. What physical constraint survives?
2. Which cult or sacred place is institutionally reconstructed here?
3. Which ancient text names the tradition?
4. Which relationship remains disputed?

## Interaction hypothesis

Use a simplified plan or axonometric view with four toggles:

- **Building:** surviving levels, porches, and rooms;
- **Cults:** Athena Polias, Poseidon-Erechtheus, Hephaistos, Boutes;
- **Tokens and neighbors:** olive/Pandroseion, salt water, trident, Kekropion;
- **Uncertainty:** damaged interiors, disputed figure identities, and source variants.

Each hotspot must show an evidence-state badge and one stable citation. The interaction should never imply that the exact ancient interior is fully recoverable.

## Distribution and product hypothesis

The recognizable Caryatids earn the open; the reveal that the on-site figures are copies and that their ancient identity is unsettled earns the first interaction. The deeper retention hook is the building's shift from “odd temple” to “many memories under one roof.” Measure episode opens, hotspot completion, evidence-layer toggles, source clicks, and Chapter 1 cross-navigation.

## Kill criteria

Stop or reframe the chapter if it:

- presents the building as a modern program of tolerance or reconciliation;
- treats Poseidon-Erechtheus as a simple character alias without cultic qualification;
- resolves Erechtheus and Erichthonius into one definitive genealogy;
- identifies the Korai with certainty or repeats Vitruvius' punishment story as fact;
- claims uninterrupted biological continuity for the present olive tree;
- reconstructs the lost interior without visible uncertainty;
- needs a product, AI, or organizational analogy to justify why the monument matters.
