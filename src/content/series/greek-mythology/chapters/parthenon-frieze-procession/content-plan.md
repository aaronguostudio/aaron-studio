# Content Plan: The Parthenon Frieze

## Voice check

**Positioning:** A curious traveler learning to read a complex ancient image without pretending its missing caption has been recovered.

**Voice rule:** Begin with concrete visual verbs—wait, turn, carry, restrain, sit—before introducing scholarly nouns. Use first person only for Aaron's stated fascination with the Parthenon sculptures and the final changed perception.

**Personal anchor:** Aaron described the Parthenon's sculptures and the way they seemed to gather the Greek gods into one world. Do not invent a specific encounter with the east block or museum display.

## Hook

Turn off the museum labels and the procession does not disappear. Horses still gather, two streams still move, offerings still approach, and larger seated figures still wait. What disappears is our certainty about what to call them.

## Core thesis

The frieze strongly evokes the Greater Panathenaia, but that conclusion is an evidence-based interpretation rather than a surviving ancient caption; its enigmatic center makes the distance between seeing and naming impossible to ignore.

## Story movement

### 1. A 160-metre sentence without its caption

- Locate the Ionic frieze high around the cella of a Doric temple.
- Establish date, scale, and continuous architectural band.
- Note that modern viewers can inspect a reassembled, eye-level digital/museum sequence that no ancient viewer saw in exactly that form.
- Evidence: F01, F02, F12.

### 2. First, describe only the marble

- West: people and horses in states of preparation.
- Long sides: riders, chariots, people, containers, musicians/objects where identification is secure enough, and sacrificial animals.
- East: slower arrival, larger seated figures, five-person cloth scene.
- Keep doubtful personal names out of this first pass.
- Evidence: F03, F04, F09.

### 3. Why the Panathenaic reading is strong

- Bring in the overall processional sequence and convergence.
- Use Plato for the festival robe and *Athenian Constitution* for later organization of procession, contests, and robe.
- Explain that these texts corroborate a ritual world; they do not identify the stone directly.
- State current broad scholarly weight without treating it as unanimity.
- Evidence: F05–F08.

### 4. The quiet puzzle at the center

- Slow the article down around the five figures and folded cloth.
- Separate visible forms from “peplos handover,” adult/child identities, direction of exchange, and ritual moment.
- Show why an apparently modest domestic-looking action can occupy the compositional center while the gods turn toward arriving streams.
- Evidence: F09, F11.

### 5. The other story the cloth might tell

- Present Connelly's Erechtheid-sacrifice interpretation accurately and briefly.
- Explain what it resolves: a mythic subject more typical of temple sculpture and a sacrificial reading of the family group.
- Explain what it asks the reader to accept: a shift to mythic time and a different identification of the same objects and people.
- Return to current balance: serious alternative, not settled replacement.
- Evidence: F10, F11.

### 6. Mortals move; gods wait

- Use relative scale and grouping to show different visual registers.
- Offer the chapter's reading: the relief does not merely illustrate a festival; it makes an ordered approach between human effort and divine presence.
- Label this as interpretation, not ancient quotation.
- Evidence: F03, F05, F15.

### 7. Look again with the labels visible

- Return to the opening sequence.
- The reader can now keep the Panathenaic name while seeing its evidence and limits.
- Close on the idea that uncertainty is not blankness; it is a visible edge around what a good explanation can support.
