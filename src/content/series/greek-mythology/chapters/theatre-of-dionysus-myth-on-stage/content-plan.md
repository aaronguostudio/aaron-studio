# Content Plan: The Theatre of Dionysus

## Voice check

**Positioning:** A careful guide entering an ancient performance through the layers that remain, while refusing to call a modern reconstruction the recovered premiere.

**Voice rule:** Favor physical sequence—sit, enter, sing, answer, vote, vanish—over abstract claims about “Western civilization.” Use first person only for a final reflection on how the season changed Aaron's way of seeing ancient sites.

**Personal anchor:** The family trip to Greece and fascination with the Acropolis motivate the series. Do not invent a specific Theatre of Dionysus visit.

## Hook

The marble seats are the easiest part to imagine and the first part we have to remove. When Aeschylus' *Oresteia* was performed in 458 BCE, the south-slope theatre was not yet the monumental stone auditorium whose remains shape the modern photograph.

## Core thesis

Athens turned inherited stories into public, competitive events, but live performance is the missing artifact; the site, festival, scripts, inscriptions, and later images let us approach it only if their dates and limits remain visible.

## Story movement

### 1. The theatre that belongs to several centuries

- Begin with stone rows, inscribed front seating, orchestra, and stage remains.
- Reveal the 350–320 BCE monumental stone phase and later Hellenistic/Roman/late-antique layers.
- Slide backward to the wooden seating and earthen orchestra reconstructed for the earlier theatre.
- Establish the rule: a beautiful ruin can preserve a place while misdating the scene in our head.
- Evidence: D01, D03–D06.

### 2. Before the play, a festival for Dionysus

- Place theatre beside the sanctuary, not as a secular building detached from cult.
- Introduce procession, sacrifice, dithyrambic and dramatic competition, sponsorship, music, and honor.
- Use the inscription as a small material trace of an organized competitive world.
- Present Aristotle's dithyramb account, then immediately state the modern origin dispute.
- Evidence: D02, D07–D09.

### 3. What changes when a myth receives bodies

- Move from “a myth says” to “an actor speaks while a chorus answers.”
- Explain that performance chooses entrances, timing, voice, gesture, spatial relations, and what an audience must wait to learn.
- Do not reconstruct exact masks or choreography yet.
- Evidence: D15, D17; interpretive transition to D18.

### 4. One performance in 458 BCE: Athena calls for a vote

- Give only the minimum Orestes background: matricide, pursuit by Erinyes, arrival under divine dispute.
- Let Athena summon jurors and announce the court in *Eumenides* 674–710.
- Show the transformation: a mobile heroic-family story ends in an explicitly Athenian space and procedure.
- Keep the hard parts present—Apollo's argument, the Erinyes' claim, gender and kinship conflict—so the scene does not become a simple triumphal “democracy replaces revenge” lesson.
- Evidence: D10–D12.

### 5. The stage did not belong only to myth

- Use Herodotus' Phrynichus story as evidence that recent collective loss could enter tragedy.
- Use Aristotle's Agathon example to show that ancient critics recognized fully invented tragic plots.
- State the narrow conclusion: traditional myth was central, not exclusive; performance was a form capable of reorganizing many kinds of story.
- Evidence: D13, D14.

### 6. The part no ruin can preserve

- Put the surviving script beside the missing performance.
- Explain the sparse/disputed stage-direction record.
- Introduce the actor statuette as a dated, bounded clue to masks and role types—not a production photograph.
- Turn on the experience's artistic layer: sound, masks, chorus pattern, fabric, gesture, daylight, crowd.
- Every added element keeps an `artistic-interpretation` label.
- Evidence: D15–D17.

### 7. When the audience leaves

- Return to the empty stone theatre.
- The article's judgment: stone holds location, text holds language, inscriptions hold institutions, but performance existed only in shared time.
- Close the Athens season by contrasting temple sculpture's stillness with theatre's disappearance.
- Evidence: D18 as explicitly editorial reading.

## Primary interaction

### “Four layers of one performance”

1. **Today / visible site** — current remains and dated later features.
2. **458 BCE / reconstructed venue** — low-detail, evidence-bounded early theatre phase.
3. **Text / *Eumenides*** — line-linked entrances, speakers, votes, and spatial references only.
4. **Staging / this interpretation** — mask, costume, chorus, sound, color, movement, and audience supplied by the project.

The reader can scrub between layers, but the UI must not blend labels during transition. At each stop, show source IDs and evidence status. A “what changed?” panel names which later architectural features have been removed and which artistic elements have been added.

## Visual role
