## Hook

The marble seats are the easiest part of ancient tragedy to imagine—and the first part the reader has to remove. When Aeschylus' *Oresteia* was performed in 458 BCE, the monumental stone auditorium whose remains shape today's photograph had not yet been built.

Within the opening, state the chapter's judgment: the stage mattered because it let a known story become a choice, a conflict and a shared event again.

## Thesis

At the City Dionysia, performance turned inherited myth into a public argument by forcing poets, actors, choruses and audiences to encounter its choices together, even though no surviving ruin, script or later image can recover the original event in full.

## Outline

### Part 1: Begin with the wrong theatre

- Open on the persuasive modern image: marble rows, inscribed front seats, orchestra and stage remains.
- Reveal that most visible monumental stone work belongs to roughly 350–320 BCE, with further Hellenistic, Roman and late-antique changes.
- Move backward to the earth-floored orchestra, timber seating evidence and reconstructed fifth-century skene.
- Establish the first reader rule: a ruin can preserve the correct place while placing the wrong century in our imagination.
- Evidence: D01, D03–D06.

### Part 2: The play was only one part of Dionysus' festival

- Place theatre beside the sanctuary and within the City Dionysia rather than presenting a secular arts venue.
- Introduce procession, sacrifice, choral performance and dramatic competition without inventing a complete day-by-day schedule.
- Use the mid-fourth-century choregic dedication as a small physical trace of sponsorship, tribe, music and victory.
- Present Aristotle's dithyramb-origin account as an ancient theory, then immediately name the sparse and disputed modern evidence.
- Evidence: D02, D07–D09.

### Part 3: A known story has to choose a body and a moment

- Shift from “myth says” to actor, chorus and audience: someone must enter, speak, wait, answer and be seen.
- Explain that performance fixes a temporary order of revelation, tone and consequence even when the underlying story is familiar.
- Do not supply a definitive mask, chorus formation, acting platform or machine effect.
- State the bounded meaning of “public”: collectively witnessed and competitively presented, not universally accessible or unanimously understood.
- Evidence: D15, D17, D18.

### Part 4: In 458 BCE, Athena asks for votes

- Give only the necessary path into *Eumenides*: Orestes' matricide, pursuit by the Erinyes and conflict among divine claims.
- Reach lines 674–710, where Athena calls the jurors to vote and announces a lasting court for Attica.
- Show what Aeschylus changes by bringing the inherited revenge story into an explicitly Athenian procedure and place.
- Preserve the difficulty of the scene: Apollo, Erinyes, kinship, gender and divine authority prevent a clean “revenge becomes democracy” moral.
- Treat links to contemporary reforms as an open scholarly question, not the article's verdict.
- Evidence: D10–D12.

### Part 5: Myth was central, not compulsory

- Use Herodotus' report about Phrynichus' *Capture of Miletus*: recent collective trauma could enter tragedy and provoke a consequential response.
- Use Aristotle's Agathon example to show that ancient criticism recognized an invented tragic plot.
- Keep both lost works tightly bounded; do not imagine staging, audience composition or prevalence from either example.
- State the narrow conclusion: tragedy was a form that could reorganize shared stories, whether inherited, recent or invented.
- Evidence: D13, D14.

### Part 6: The script is not the performance

- Place a surviving passage beside everything it does not preserve: timbre, music, exact gesture, masks, chorus pattern, tempo and audience reaction.
- Explain the handful of possible transmitted stage directions and the scholarly dispute about original performance copies.
- Introduce the late-fifth-/early-fourth-century actor statuette only as a dated clue to a wider mask and role tradition, probably closer to comic types—not an *Eumenides* costume.
- Turn on the project's staging layer and disclose every supplied choice.
- Evidence: D15–D17.

### Part 7: When everyone leaves, what remains?

- Return to the empty, multi-period theatre.
- Distill the material record without turning it into a slogan: stone preserves place and phases; text preserves words; inscriptions preserve institutions; objects preserve fragments of a visual tradition.
- State the article's judgment one final time: performance made myth publicly contestable precisely because it happened in shared time and then disappeared.
- Close the Athens season by contrasting the stillness of temple sculpture with a story that had to be enacted again.
