# Canon Note — Chapter 6

## Canonical Idea

**Performance makes inherited myth publicly contestable.** At the City Dionysia, a poet, actors, chorus, judges, and assembled spectators encountered a story in shared and competitive time. “Public” describes that performance condition; it does not establish universal attendance, equal participation, democratic instruction, or consensus.

The defining artifact is also the one that does not survive: the original performance. Architecture, texts, inscriptions, and theatrical objects approach it from different dates and directions without becoming a complete recording.

## Reusable Frame

For any reconstruction of ancient performance, inspect four layers separately:

1. **site phase** — which architecture belongs to the performance date;
2. **festival institution** — what cult, competition, sponsorship, and organization can be supported;
3. **dramatic text** — what words, speakers, and actions survive or can be cautiously inferred;
4. **this staging** — which mask, movement, sound, color, crowd, and response the modern work supplies.

The four layers can interact, but the interface must not blend their labels.

## Claims Added

- Today's Theatre of Dionysus is a palimpsest; much visible monumental stone belongs to the 350–320 BCE rebuilding and later alteration, not the 458 BCE *Oresteia* environment.
- Aristotle preserves an important ancient dithyramb-origin theory, while modern scholarship treats tragedy's beginnings as deeply disputed.
- In *Eumenides*, Athena brings a cycle of vengeance into an Athenian trial and voting scene; this is a textual case, not proof of one political message.
- Traditional myth was central to tragedy but not mandatory; Aristotle's Agathon example supplies a narrow counterexample.
- The transmitted scripts do not preserve a complete modern prompt book, and dated actor objects do not authenticate one named premiere.

## Claims Updated

- The season's evidence discipline now includes time-based media: absence can mean a vanished event rather than a broken object.
- Myth is not only reconstructed by modern readers; ancient dramatists also reorganized inherited stories through performance choices.
- Athena's conflict-solving role links Chapter 1 and Chapter 6, but no stable personality theory should be inferred across separate stories and media.
- “Civic” and “public” are now explicitly separated from claims of democratic outcome.
