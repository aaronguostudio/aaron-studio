# Red-Team Review — The Theatre of Dionysus

## Issue 1 — The opening ruin can place Aeschylus in the wrong building

**Risk:** Marble seating is so visually persuasive that readers may carry the later stone theatre into the 458 BCE case study even after one caveat.

**Required revision:** Make the chronological mismatch the hook and repeat the removal rule before the visual handoff.

**Revision completed:** Part 1 distinguishes earth/timber, fifth-century skene, 350–320 BCE stone rebuilding, and later layers. Part 6 requires discrete `Today` and `458 BCE` states.

## Issue 2 — “Public argument” could mean “democratic classroom”

**Risk:** Public performance may be conflated with universal attendance, equal citizenship, direct political participation, or one civic lesson.

**Required revision:** Define “public” as a performance condition and separate staged jurors from spectators.

**Revision completed:** The introduction excludes equal attendance and a single democratic lesson. Part 4 now says spectators watched a represented jury; they did not become Orestes' jurors or acquire a common verdict.

## Issue 3 — One play could be asked to represent all tragedy

**Risk:** *Eumenides* is unusually Athenian in its ending and cannot support a universal account of the genre.

**Required revision:** Name it as one mechanism case and add a counterexample to “every tragedy retold myth.”

**Revision completed:** The article narrows its claim to what Aeschylus does in this play. Part 5 uses Aristotle's Agathon example to establish that inherited myth was central, not compulsory.

## Issue 4 — The origin story could become linear

**Risk:** Aristotle's dithyramb account may be repeated as settled history: Dionysian ritual became tragedy.

**Required revision:** Keep the later ancient theory beside the modern evidence warning.

**Revision completed:** Part 2 attributes the account to Aristotle, dates its evidentiary position, and immediately gives Scodel's disputed-origin boundary.

## Issue 5 — Surviving text could be treated as a production record

**Risk:** A complete play can create confidence that original blocking, pace, masks, and music are recoverable.

**Required revision:** Make the performance gap part of the mechanism rather than a closing disclaimer.

**Revision completed:** Part 3 explains the sparse and disputed stage-direction record before the *Eumenides* case; Part 6 inventories what every staging still supplies.

## Issue 6 — Cross-period objects could authenticate a premiere

**Risk:** A mask or actor image from a later date or different performance tradition may be read as an illustration of 458 BCE tragedy.

**Required revision:** Use at most one dated material example and state what it cannot show.

**Revision completed:** The Met statuette is labeled late-fifth-/early-fourth-century and more closely connected to comic role traditions; it is explicitly not an *Eumenides* portrait or costume.

## Issue 7 — Festival description could overstate audience and schedule

**Risk:** Smooth prose may invent exact attendance, a fixed multi-day timetable, judging rules, or a modern secular festival identity.

**Required revision:** Describe only the securely supported relationships and use the choregic dedication within its fourth-century, dithyrambic scope.

**Revision completed:** Part 2 avoids capacity and day counts and says directly that the inscription is not a fifth-century tragic program or complete schedule.

## Issue 8 — Personal narration could imply a theatre visit

**Risk:** A seat-level travel scene would exceed Aaron's stated memory.

**Required revision:** Anchor the chapter in the family Greece trip and the season's changed way of seeing, not a fabricated moment at this site.

**Revision completed:** The only first-person return appears in the final section and makes no claim that Aaron entered or sat in the theatre.

## Substantive revision completed

The complete draft initially defined “public” in the opening but left the trial scene open to a democratic-participation reading. The final version adds a separate paragraph after Athena's vote: the spectators watched a jury represented on stage; they were not themselves that jury. This narrows the thesis at its strongest example and changes the article's political implication.

## Decision

**PASS for prose polish.** Architecture, festival, case-study scope, non-mythic boundary, public-performance claim, and reconstruction limits are all source-bounded. No new evidence is needed for this draft.
